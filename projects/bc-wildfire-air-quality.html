BC Wildfire & Air Quality Analytics

Relating large wildfire activity to daily PM2.5 across British Columbia (2022–2024).

Overview

Around Summer, the sun starts looking very red and the horizon becomes translucent. It is smoke! But now, are these itty-bitty air pollutants all from wildfires? Which wildfire could it possibly be from? Can we predict when the smokes are going to cover our neighbourhood by looking at recent fire activity near? I have built an analysis pipeline to join hourly air-quality monitoring data with large-fire records and ask whether recent fire activity near a station aligns with higher daily pollution.

The work is published as a Quarto website with exploratory analysis, supervised models, clustering, and a short learning module on data-auditing mistakes encountered along the way.

What I Did

  • Audited and explored NAPS hourly PM2.5 data and CNFD large-fire records.
  • Aggregated valid daily PM2.5 per station and engineered spatial–temporal wildfire exposure features.
  • Ran EDA on year-to-year PM2.5 patterns and fire proximity associations.
  • Built KNN classification, KNN/linear regression, and K-means clustering workflows with tidymodels.
  • Documented the full pipeline in Quarto and added an educational module from analysis mistakes.

Key Results

  • Stations with more recent nearby large fires tended to show higher daily PM2.5 in exploratory analysis.
  • Wildfire proximity features carried signal, but predictive models had limited performance for forecasting daily PM2.5.
  • Clustering highlighted distinct combinations of exposure and pollution conditions across station-days.

Technical Approach

Hourly PM2.5 from NAPS were grouped and summarized to provide daily station means when enough hours were observed. Wildfire records were cleaned and linked geographically, then summarized into recent-fire exposure features for each station-day. Quarto organized reproducible chapters from raw auditing through modeling. tidymodels handled resampling and tuning so comparisons between KNN and linear regression were consistent.

Challenge

Joining two messy real-world sources meant small date and geography mistakes could easily distort exposure features. Auditing early—and turning those failures into the learning module—made the later modeling more trustworthy even when prediction remained difficult.

Limitations

Since the data was missing wind direction, intensity, and other factors that could contribute to the exposure of PM2.5 and the movement of smoke, the predictions were unreliable.
